Tessera · a PM case study

Help creators apply to campaigns they can win, and brands find them faster.

Prepared by Alisha ChaudhariFocus: Campaign discovery & matchingNov 2026

Problem framing, a core-loop MVP, a PRD-lite and one experiment randomised by campaign, built so the result shows whether more paid work actually gets done.

Tessera, its brands and creators are fictional. Every number marked A is my assumption, with a week-1 check.

01 · Problem discovery & framing

Start with what creators are actually trying to do

Before listing problems, I mapped what a mid-size creator needs from a marketplace, and what they're afraid of. These are hypotheses to test in interviews, not findings.

Predictable income

A steady pipeline of paid work, not one lucky win a month.

months where nothing comes through

Their time respected

A good pitch takes ~20 min. 12 pitches is ~4 hours of unpaid work for 1 win A.

pitching into a void

Fair pay

Knowing before applying whether the budget covers their rate. Many micro creators aren't sure what their rate should be.

being lowballed, or underpricing out of desperation

Audience trust

Promoting brands that suit their followers. Their audience is their whole business.

looking like a sell-out

Treated as a professional

A real decision with a reason, on a known date, from a brand that pays on time.

silence, then seeing someone else's post

Room to grow

Knowing what would get them into bigger campaigns.

staying stuck without knowing why
The insight behind everything below: for a creator, an application is unpaid work and a silent rejection is a small blow to their confidence. My guess A is that a marketplace that makes them apply blind slowly loses them, and brands are left with a thinner pool.

Where it breaks in the lifecycle

Two problems, not three

P1 and P2 are one problem seen from two sides: Tessera never captures who fits what. P3 is a separate problem. I show P1 and P2 apart only because each side feels it differently.

P1

Creator side: applying blind, hearing nothing back

Who
Creators, especially micro creators (10K–100K), who are most of the supply
Where
Finding campaigns → applying → waiting
What happens
The feed is newest-first. Follower minimums, audience and city requirements are buried in free-text briefs, so creators apply to campaigns they can't win. Rejections come late or never, with no reason.
Why it matters
My guess A: creators stop applying after 2–3 silent rejections. If that's true, supply thins out in exactly the niches brands need.
How I'd check it
Hand-tag 200 recent applications for missed requirements · return-to-apply rate after 1, 2 and 3 straight rejections · 12 creator interviews.
P2

Brand side: drowning in applicants who don't fit

Who
Brand marketing managers at D2C brands, often a team of one or two
Where
Reviewing applicants → selecting creators
What happens
~70 unranked applicants per campaign A. To judge fit, the manager opens each Instagram profile, looks at their content and asks for audience screenshots. It takes 4–6 hours; median time to a shortlist is 6 days A.
Why it matters
Revenue is commission on completed work. ~45% of campaigns don't fill every slot within 14 days of closing A, and my guess A is that brands with a slow first campaign rarely run a second.
How I'd check it
Launch-to-shortlist time · profile views per applicant · repeat rate for brands whose first campaign under-filled · 6 brand interviews.
P3

A separate problem: creators can't see when they'll be paid

Who
Creators who have won and delivered, plus the support team
Where
Content approval → payout
What happens
Payout starts only when the brand approves content, which has no deadline or visible status. ~30% wait more than 14 days A.
Why it matters
Damages trust among the creators who already succeed, and creates support load. But it only touches creators who were selected, about a quarter of active creators each quarter A.
How I'd check it
Approval-to-payout delays · payment support tickets · whether top creators who waited long stopped applying.

Problem statement

Creators can't tell which campaigns they can win, and brands can't tell which applicants fit.

Because Tessera doesn't capture or show fit, creators spend unpaid hours on applications they can't win, and brands take a week to shortlist and leave slots unfilled.

02 · Feature proposal

Fit-first matching

Of the five focus areas, discovery & matching is the one that fixes the shared root cause behind P1 and P2.

Focus areaSolvesWhy / why not now
Discovery & matching chosenP1 + P2Fixes the shared root cause. Every campaign and every active creator feels it. Buildable with simple rules.
Payout transparency nextP3High pain, narrower reach. The real fix needs a finance-side approval deadline first.

Target users

Primary: micro creators (10K–100K) in skincare, food, fashion and wellness. Most of the supply, most hurt by blind applying, most likely to churn.

Secondary: brand marketing managers at D2C brands running 1–3 campaigns a quarter, usually with no agency. They want content that performs and fits their look, and a shortlist fast enough to hit their launch date.

Internal: Creator Ops, who hand-match today and would own tuning the rules.

The core loop: six things in v1, and the outcome each is for

1

Structured brief v1

Niche, deliverables, budget range, follower minimum, audience age, gender and location, slots and deadline as fields instead of free text.

Requirements become something the product can filter and rank on
2

Structured creator profile v1

Up to 3 niches and a rate per deliverable, with a suggested range by size and niche for creators who don't know their rate A. Audience split and engagement are copied from Instagram Insights, labelled "self-reported", and verified only once a brand shortlists them.

Brands stop asking for screenshots (measure: screenshot requests per campaign)
3

"You qualify" / "Not eligible yet" v1

Campaigns a creator can't apply to move into their own tab, with the exact gap, like "1.8K followers short". A brand can still invite a near-miss, so a careless minimum isn't absolute.

Fewer wasted applications; a rejection becomes a goal
4

Match score with reasons v1

0–100 with a label (Strong / Good / Stretch), the main reason and the weakest point in plain words. Both sides see the same breakdown. The brand's list also shows each creator's 3 latest posts, because brands choose on look and tone, which the score doesn't judge.

Creators apply where they can win; brands shortlist in minutes
5

A decision by a set date v1

Brands shortlist, or decline with one of five reasons. Applications still undecided 5 days after closing close automatically, and that counts against the brand: every campaign card shows the brand's response rate, typical response time and paid-on-time %.

Brands have a reason to decide; creators can choose brands that answer
6

Invite strong creators v1

A "Suggested" list of strong matches who haven't applied, with one-click invites. Capped at 5 open invites per creator per week A, and creators can mark themselves unavailable, so the same top creators aren't flooded.

Campaigns fill when applicants are thin, without overbooking the top creators
Not in v1, on purpose: live count of qualifying creators while writing the brief · warning when the budget is below market · "notify me when I qualify" · Instagram auto-sync for every creator · YouTube. Each is reasonable, but none is needed to test whether fit drives filled slots.

Fairness choices, and the gaps I know about
  • New creators aren't punished: no history scores a neutral 4/5, not 0
  • Track record is only 5 points, so one late delivery doesn't lock someone out
  • Location only counts when the brand asks for it, so smaller-city creators aren't marked down by default
  • Creators see exactly what brands see. No hidden ranking.
  • Known gap: content style. Brands mostly choose on look and tone, which the score doesn't judge. The latest posts sit next to the score, and the week-2 back-test is where this would show.
  • Known gap: self-reported data. Audience and engagement are self-reported until shortlist, so the score is only as honest as the profile until then.
  • Open question: a visible "Stretch" label may discourage creators. Design tests label-only vs number before launch.

03 · PRD lite

Fit-first matching, v1

Team assumption A: 1 PM, 1 designer, 3 engineers, an analyst part-time, and Creator Ops. v1 is only the core loop.

What v1 must achieve

Goals

  • More slots filled per campaign, so more paid work gets done
  • Creators apply mainly to campaigns they can win
  • Brands shortlist from a ranked list in under 3 days
  • Applications get a reasoned brand decision; brands that don't decide are visible to creators
Deliberately out of scope

Non-goals

  • AI or machine-learned matching
  • Chat and price negotiation
  • Changing commission or pricing (see the R9 note)
  • Content review and approval workflow
  • Payout tracking (next quarter)
  • Platforms beyond Instagram

Key requirements, as one journey

Read left to right, then follow the arrow down and back. Each box is one requirement in plain words; the tag says who does it.

The small print, in plain words
  • R1: a campaign stays open for at least 3 days.
  • R2: numbers the creator types in are marked "self-reported" until they're checked.
  • R3: if verified numbers are more than 20% off, the score is recalculated with the real numbers, the brand sees the new score with a "numbers updated" note and decides whether to keep the creator on the shortlist, and the creator is told why.
  • R5: the score is locked when the creator applies, so it can't change while the brand reviews. If two creators tie, better audience fit wins, then whoever applied first.
  • R6: the pitch is 80–600 characters. Quoting above the budget is allowed, with a warning.
  • R9: I'm assuming A Tessera charges brands 15% today. Fixing the offer at the creator's quote would be a pricing change, so it needs its own decision.
  • R10: a creator gets at most 5 open invites a week A and can mark themselves unavailable. A brand with no history shows as "New brand".

Why win rate isn't the primary metric: blocking applications that would have lost removes them from the bottom of the ratio, so win rate rises even if no extra creator gets paid. Filled slots can only go up if more work actually happens.

Is +0.6 realistic? A range, not a promise

The ceiling

About 2 of ~6 slots go empty per campaign today A. Matching fully addresses slow decisions (~35% of under-fill) and partly addresses too few qualified creators (~25%). It doesn't fix low budgets or offer declines.

So matching can reach roughly 0.7–1.0 of those 2 empty slots. A lift of +0.6 would mean fixing most of what's reachable.

So I plan for a range

Expected lift: +0.3 to +0.8 filled slots (4.3–4.8). +0.6 is only realistic if week 1 shows slow decisions are the biggest cause of under-fill.

If the week-1 split shows budget or declines dominate, I'd lower the expectation before building, not after the test.

Guardrails & sizing

Top 3 guardrails (must not get worse)
  • Applications per campaign stay at 25+
  • Offer acceptance rate drops by no more than 5 points
  • Win concentration: share of wins going to the top 10% of creators rises by no more than 5 points

Also tracked: new creators' share of applications, average fee per deliverable, share of accepted offers that end in completed content.

Rough sizing A

Only count slots that would otherwise go unfilled. ~160 campaigns a month × +0.3 to +0.8 filled slots ≈ 50–130 more filled slots, or ~45–120 completed collaborations after the usual drop-off (the North Star target of ~645–720). At ~₹28K actually paid per slot (not budget), that's ≈ ₹13–34L more completed work a month.

At 15% commission: ≈ ₹2–5L more a month (~₹3.5L at the +0.55 rollout bar). Partly-filled campaigns already earn commission, which is why this is per slot, not per campaign.

The A/B test, randomised by campaign

In plain words: what we're testing

The question: if creators and brands can both see who fits a campaign, do more creators end up with paid work?

How: for 8 weeks, every new campaign is randomly put into one of two groups when it launches, like a coin flip.

  • Test group (the new way): only creators who meet the requirements can apply. Each sees a match score with the reason. The brand gets a ranked list, must decide within 5 days, and can invite strong creators who didn't apply.
  • Control group (today's way): the campaign runs exactly as it does now. Anyone can apply, campaigns show newest first, there's no score and no deadline.

What we compare: how many of each campaign's slots end up filled by a creator who accepted an offer. Example: Kora Botanics wants 6 creators. Campaigns like it fill about 4 slots today. If test campaigns fill about 4.6 on average and control campaigns about 4.0, the new way gets more creators paid. Across ~160 campaigns in each group, a gap of about half a slot (+0.55) or more is big enough to trust.

Why split campaigns, not people: a campaign gets the new flow on both sides at once, so the creator applying and the brand choosing see the same thing. If we split creators instead, a brand would see a mix of old and new applicants, and we couldn't tell whether the brand-side changes helped.

Test details
Hypothesis
When both sides see fit (gating, ranking with reasons, a decision deadline, capped invites), campaigns fill more slots than with today's flow.
Unit
Campaigns, split 50/50 at launch. Test campaigns get the full experience on both sides; control campaigns run as today.
Primary metric
Filled slots per campaign, 14 days after close.
Length & power
8 weeks ≈ 320 campaigns A. With a spread of about 1.8 slots between campaigns A, the smallest lift we can reliably detect is about +0.55. Extending to 12 weeks (~480 campaigns) brings that down to about +0.45.
If extended
Roll out at +0.45 or more at 12 weeks. Below that, I treat it as no clear lift and go back to the week-1 under-fill split, rather than extending again.
Rollout bar
Expected +0.3 to +0.8. Roll out at +0.55 or more, the smallest lift 8 weeks can confirm.
Bundled
Gating, ranking, deadline and invites ship together, so a win won't say which one mattered. A follow-up creator-feed test would separate ranking from gating.
Analysis
At campaign level, so repeat applications from the same creator don't inflate the result. Guardrails that involve creators (new creators' share, win concentration) compare applications to test vs control campaigns, clustered by creator.
Spillover
Creators see test and control campaigns in the same feed. I'd check control campaigns' application volume didn't fall.
05 · Trade-offs & prioritisation

What I didn't build, and why

A team only has so much time. Here's what I left out, where the work would go, and what I'd do if the team were half the size.

Left out on purpose

AI matching

We don't yet have enough past results to teach an AI what a good match looks like. Simple rules test the idea now, anyone can understand them, and they collect the data an AI would need later.

Chat and price negotiation

Someone would have to watch the chats, and it makes it easy to take the deal outside Tessera. Whether the price is fixed at the creator's quote is a separate pricing decision.

Paid boosts for creators

It would charge the creators who are already struggling, and brands would stop trusting the ranking.

Content approval workflow

This happens after creators are chosen, so it doesn't help campaigns fill up.

Payout tracker

Important, and next in line. But finance first needs to agree a deadline for brands to approve content.

v1.1 extras

Live count of matching creators, a low-budget warning, "tell me when I qualify", automatic Instagram sync, YouTube. Only if the first version works.

If we only had half the engineers

Half the team (1.5 engineers) for the same 6 weeks ≈ 9 weeks of work. The plan below is 9.5, so the brand's track record moves to the beta week if we run short.

I'd still build the whole loop, just simpler:

  • Campaign form and creator profile; creators type in their own numbers (3.5 weeks)
  • The "Can I apply?" check that shows what's missing (1.5 weeks)
  • A simple ordered list with a label like "Strong match", but no number (1.5 weeks)
  • Brands must give a reason and decide by a date (1.5 weeks)
  • Invites, with a weekly limit (1 week)
  • The brand's track record on campaign cards, so creators can see which brands let applications close without a decision (0.5 week)

Dropped: the 0–100 score and its breakdown · automatic Instagram checks (Ops checks only creators about to get an offer: ~4 per campaign, ~640 a month at ~7 minutes each, about 75 hours, roughly half an Ops person A) · recent posts on the list.

What we lose: brands get less proof, and creators see a label instead of a full explanation. That's fine for a first test, because all we need to learn is whether showing fit fills more slots.

Bonus · Alternative I rejected

Ops hand-picks creators for every campaign

The idea

Creator Ops picks 10 creators per campaign and sends the list to the brand. Quick to start, high quality, brands love it.

Why I said no
  • It doesn't scale. ~2 hours × 160 campaigns a month ≈ 320 hours, about 2 full-time people, growing with every campaign A. By contrast, v1's manual checks cover only the ~15% of shortlisted creators who can't connect Instagram: ~145 checks at ~7 minutes, about 16 hours a month A.
  • It takes choice away from creators. Only creators Ops already knows get picked.
  • It hides the problem instead of fixing it, and teaches us nothing.

I'd keep it as a premium service for the top 20 brands, with Ops using the same scores to work faster.
